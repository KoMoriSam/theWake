In my first year at university, I read several articles on cultural exchanges between Sri Lanka and ancient China for an introductory course in my major and a survey course on South Asia. Most approached the subject through history, religion or politics. Few really developed their arguments from archaeological material.

Later, while borrowing books from the department, I was immediately drawn to Jia Xinghe's doctoral dissertation, *Cultural Exchanges between Sri Lanka and Ancient China: A Study Centered on Excavated Chinese Ceramics*. The dissertation passed its defense in 2011. I wanted to know: what would this history look like if we started with excavated ceramics, coins and inscriptions, rather than relying only on historical texts, accounts of envoys and religious sources?

## Starting with the author

My first question on seeing the cover was: who is Jia Xinghe?

I have a habit of learning about an author before reading their work. The book's back cover introduces him as follows:

> Jia Xinghe is an archaeological specialist at Sri Lanka's national archaeological institute. He studied at the University of Science and Technology of China and Sun Yat-sen University, obtaining a master's degree and a doctorate. His research focuses on scientific and humanistic approaches to the archaeology of ceramics excavated in Sri Lanka. He has published extensively in specialist journals including *Archaeology and Cultural Relics* and *Rock and Mineral Analysis*.[^1]

Searching the Chinese internet for “Jia Xinghe” yielded little. The main results were three papers he published while studying in China, all concerning Sri Lankan and Chinese archaeology.

I had initially assumed that “Jia Xinghe” was a Chinese name he adopted after arriving in China. Only after browsing many webpages did I discover that his Sinhala name is ප්‍රියන්ත ජයසිංහ, Priyantha Jayasinghe. The surname Jayasinha or Jayasinghe can itself be rendered phonetically as “Jia Xinghe”; it was not an unrelated new name.

With his Sinhala name, I tried searching again. In the end, I found only a short online article by him, also about the Maritime Silk Road and China–Sri Lanka relations, along with a few news items from Sri Lanka's archaeological authorities. The information was so scattered that I gave up “excavating the author” and began reading the book instead.

## What is the book about?

Although the title emphasizes excavated Chinese ceramics, the book's scope is considerably broader.

Chapters Two and Four mainly address cultural exchanges between ancient China and Sri Lanka and the related archaeological evidence. Chapter Three discusses the archaeological value of the Maritime Silk Road, while Chapter Five considers Sri Lanka's position in ancient Chinese maritime connections. Ceramics provide an important thread, but the author also examines ports, coins, inscriptions, Buddhist objects, silk and gemstones.

Given my current knowledge and reading ability, I found the connections between these parts rather loose. The book covers cultural exchanges, archaeological discoveries, shifting sea routes and historical assessments, but often moves abruptly between them. After reading it, I understood what material the author had collected without immediately seeing a clear line of argument.

This report is therefore difficult to organize as a chapter-by-chapter account. I can only begin with a few issues that interest me and discuss them as I go.

## Ancient exchanges through ceramics, coins and inscriptions

Chapter Four is the core of the book.

It introduces a range of Sri Lankan archaeological material that Chinese readers may not know: ceramics, coins, inscriptions and other excavated objects. Much of the pottery consists of Tang- and Song-period Chinese ceramics found at Mantai. The coins are mainly Song copper cash. The inscriptions chiefly relate to Zheng He's Ming-period voyages. Other materials include silk, Buddhist objects and gemstones.

These objects do not tell their own histories. Archaeologists must establish their dates, origins, routes of circulation and contexts of use, then compare finds from different places.

Why did a piece of Chinese porcelain end up in a Sri Lankan port? Did Chinese merchants bring it directly, or did it pass through Southeast Asian, South Indian or Arab traders? Did local people use it as an everyday vessel, a trade commodity, or a symbol of status and wealth? If West Asian pottery, Indian coins and locally made objects were found at the same site, how were they related?

By tracing the origins of such material, Jia Xinghe tries to reconstruct aspects of ancient Indian Ocean trade. Compared with historical accounts centered on dynasties, envoys and tribute, excavated objects preserve a different history: ships docked, goods changed hands, and merchants, monks and sailors stayed in ports. Many of these people never entered official histories, but the objects they handled survived at archaeological sites.

Chapter Four mentions Mantai repeatedly. Curious, I looked up some additional material.

Sri Lanka's coastline has many ports. Well-known early ports include Mantai (මහ තිත්ථ, “great port”), Jaffna (යාපනය), Trincomalee (ත්‍රිකුණාමලය) and Galle (ගාල්ල). Chinese ceramics have been found at these ports. Material from the northern ports is relatively early, with some finds dating to the Tang and Song periods.

The Mantai site lies in the Mannar region of northwestern Sri Lanka, near the Gulf of Mannar and across Palk Bay from southeastern India. It occupied an important position on Indian Ocean routes and was one of the ancient capital Anuradhapura's gateways to the sea. Anuradhapura served for a long period as Sri Lanka's political center between 337 BCE and 1017 CE. Mantai connected the capital to Indian Ocean trading networks and was once a well-known port.[^2]

The *Mahavamsa* also records that the Indian prince Vijaya (විජය රජ) and his followers first landed in the Mantai area when they reached Lanka.[^3] Whatever legendary elements this account contains, it shows that Mantai holds a special place in Sri Lanka's historical memory.

Seven years after Jia Xinghe completed his doctorate in China, an archaeological team from the Shanghai Museum traveled to Sri Lanka in August 2018 for forty days of joint archaeological work. Originally planned to continue for three to five years, the project investigated Chinese artifacts, ancient ports and shipwrecks, among other subjects.[^4]

Such joint archaeology can address very concrete questions. Does the distribution of Chinese ceramics across Sri Lankan ports differ by period? In which layers are particular ceramic types concentrated? Do the rise and decline of ports correspond to shifts in shipping routes? Can trade recorded in written sources be corroborated at archaeological sites?

Historical documents provide people, dates and events; archaeological material can supplement or even correct them. Even for periods with written records, the history of civilization still needs archaeology to offer reference points, corroboration, enrichment and refinement.

Mantai is quiet today. The ships, goods and crowds that once passed through its harbor are long gone, but potsherds, coins and architectural remains survive. They do not necessarily record grand diplomatic events; more often, they preserve traces of everyday trade. Through them, exchanges between China and Sri Lanka become more than visits by envoys in history books: they become a past that can be approached through tangible objects.

As for drawing a direct line from ancient Mantai to today's Hambantota Port or Colombo Port City, I now think caution is necessary. The political circumstances, trading institutions and technologies of ancient and modern ports differ enormously. Simple analogies will not do. Yet today's ports also leave buildings, documents, goods and traces of human movement. People centuries from now may likewise depend on such material to understand our time.

## From archaeological material back to bilateral history

Without archaeological training, I find it difficult to assess the book's artifact classifications, dating or scientific testing methods professionally. Several historical episodes in Chapter Two are more familiar to me: tribute records between Lanka and China during the Han dynasty, Buddhist exchanges from the Han through the Tang, and Zheng He's Ming voyages.

These largely belong to the ancient history of China–Sri Lanka relations. The book also mentions more recent relations, but only briefly. To fill that gap, I read Tong Jiameng's *Sri Lanka under Colonial Rule*. Its focus is likewise not bilateral relations, but it offers ways into understanding modern Sri Lankan politics and diplomacy.

After reading both books, I began to wonder: what has sustained relations between China and Sri Lanka from ancient times into the modern era?

## Ancient relations: the material conditions behind cultural exchange

Marx observed that everything people struggle to obtain is connected with their interests.[^5]

In *On New Democracy*, Mao Zedong likewise discussed the close relationship between culture and a society's political and economic conditions: the economy forms the foundation, while politics is its concentrated expression.[^6]

Applied to ancient China–Sri Lanka relations, these ideas suggest that cultural exchanges did not occur independently of material circumstances. The spread of Buddhism, journeys by monks, visits by envoys and trade all required relatively stable political conditions, navigable routes, economic resources for long-distance activity and actual needs on both sides.

Relations between ancient China and Sri Lanka initially took the form of religious and personal exchanges, then gradually developed more stable trading connections. China wanted spices, gemstones, pearls and goods from farther west. Sri Lanka wanted ceramics, silk and copper cash, while using its ports to participate in Indian Ocean trade. Both therefore had reasons to keep the relationship going.

Jia Xinghe points out that Sri Lanka often acted as an entrepôt on the Maritime Silk Road. In many cases, ancient Chinese merchants' ultimate trading partners were in Arab and Roman regions farther west.[^7]

But being an entrepôt did not mean being unimportant. Precisely because Sri Lanka stood on major Indian Ocean routes, ships could dock, replenish supplies, trade and reorganize cargo there. It was not necessarily the place of production or final destination of every shipment, yet it could shape the routes goods followed and bring together merchants, religions and languages from different regions.

Ancient China and Sri Lanka differed in national power, economic scale and political institutions. They were far apart and rarely stood at the center of each other's diplomacy. Their relationship endured because each could gain practical benefits, rather than because their aims were always identical.

Perhaps partly because mountains and oceans separated them, ancient China paid relatively limited attention to South Asia, and the two seldom became entangled in prolonged direct conflict. Apart from the exceptional war with the kingdom in Ceylon during Zheng He's voyages,[^8] exchanges were generally peaceful.

Put more casually, they were something like “silk-trade buddies”: not constantly in touch, but meeting when a ship passed, goods needed exchanging or monks traveled between them. Without a specific reason, neither had to think about the other all the time.

It was hardly a dramatic relationship, but it lasted.

Jia Xinghe writes: “Mountains cannot obstruct friendly exchanges between China and Sri Lanka, nor can the sea sever the close ties between their peoples.”[^9] The Chinese phrasing is a little stiff, but his affection for these exchanges is clear. For a Sri Lankan scholar who has spent years studying archaeological material connecting the two countries, that feeling probably also comes from personal experience.

## Modern relations: adjustments within an overall stable relationship

Tong Jiameng characterizes ancient relations between China and Sri Lanka as principally “peaceful and mutually beneficial cultural exchange.”[^10] In modern times, relations have remained broadly stable, but their substance has changed.

Ancient exchanges mainly depended on merchant ships, monks and envoys. Modern interstate relations involve diplomatic recognition, government agreements, international conferences, trade, infrastructure and party politics. More actors participate, and their interests are more complicated.

Sri Lanka recognized the legitimacy of the People's Republic of China before the two formally established diplomatic relations. Subsequent relations included “elephant diplomacy,” the Bandung Conference, the Rubber–Rice Pact and Belt and Road cooperation. Each arose in a different international setting; they cannot simply be arranged along a straight line of “ever-deepening friendship.”

When the Rubber–Rice Pact was signed, China needed rubber and Ceylon needed rice. Agreement was possible first because each had a definite need. The pact has been recalled repeatedly not only because it makes a friendly story, but because the cooperation actually addressed practical problems at the time.

In the twenty-first century, cooperation has increasingly centered on ports, transport and urban development. Changes of governing parties, fiscal conditions and policy in Sri Lanka also affect projects. Colombo Port City was suspended in March 2015 and later resumed following review and negotiation. In May 2021, Sri Lanka's parliament passed the Colombo Port City Economic Commission Bill, opening a new stage for the project.[^11]

An overall stable relationship therefore does not mean that individual projects run smoothly on their own. They must contend with local law, domestic politics, debt pressures, public opinion and environmental issues. Reading every adjustment as a setback to friendship is inaccurate; portraying cooperation as an entirely uncontested success story is equally simplistic.

The joint statement issued by the People's Republic of China and the Democratic Socialist Republic of Sri Lanka on 20 October 2023 described their relationship as a strategic cooperative partnership based on sincere mutual assistance and enduring friendship, and as an example of friendly coexistence and mutually beneficial cooperation between countries of different sizes.

That is the relationship's official description. Researchers still need to ask: in which areas does “mutual benefit” materialize for each side? Do different governments, businesses and ordinary people experience cooperation in the same way? How do national narratives of friendship translate into employment, education, port operations and community life?

These questions are harder than simply deciding whether relations are “good,” but they are closer to reality.

As a student of Sinhala, there is little I can do individually. I certainly cannot determine the course of bilateral relations. But I can begin by reading primary material, understanding local society and using the language accurately. Only by engaging with Sri Lanka's historical and present circumstances can I reduce distance and avoid taking too much for granted.

## Questions that remained after reading

My first response to *Cultural Exchanges between Sri Lanka and Ancient China: A Study Centered on Excavated Chinese Ceramics* was not that the questions had been answered. Instead, there were many more things I wanted to pursue.

Who mainly used the Chinese ceramics found at Sri Lankan sites? Did their arrival alter local consumption habits? Do the kinds of excavated ceramics differ between ports, cities and religious sites of different status?

Can “tribute” and “sending envoys” in historical texts really be matched to the circulation of goods revealed by archaeology? Did official envoys and private merchants follow the same routes? What political entities did names such as “Lion Kingdom” and “Xilanshan” in Chinese histories designate at different times?

Looking further ahead, what can we compare between ancient and modern ports, and what cannot be compared? Treating Mantai, Hambantota and Colombo Port City as continuations of one historical tradition simply because all lie on maritime routes can easily obscure their very different institutions and international settings.

These questions also remind me that China–Sri Lanka relations cannot be understood through politics, economics or culture alone. Studying ancient exchanges requires historical texts, archaeological reports, religious records and navigational sources together. Studying modern relations requires policy documents, investment data, media reporting and responses within local society. More evidence does not necessarily make conclusions simpler; it may reveal more contradictions.

That is probably where research really begins.

## Closing thoughts

Jia Xinghe's book gave me my first concentrated encounter with Chinese ceramics, copper coins and inscriptions excavated in Sri Lanka. Previously, my reading on bilateral exchanges had made me more familiar with Faxian, Zheng He, tribute and the spread of Buddhism. Archaeological material directed my attention toward ports, cargo and ordinary people's activities.

These apparently silent objects can correct impressions created by written sources. Histories often revolve around rulers, envoys and major events. Potsherds left at archaeological sites remind us that overseas exchanges also took place in merchant ships' holds, port warehouses, offerings on temple altars and residents' everyday lives.

Reading this book does not mean that I have mastered the history of China–Sri Lanka relations. Archaeological methods remain unfamiliar, and my understanding of much of the material is still superficial. In seeking further research, I need to broaden my reading and compare the evidence and questions used by different disciplines.

In *On Practice*, Mao Zedong writes:

> “Without stepping outside his gate, the scholar knows all that happens under heaven” was an empty saying in ancient times, when technology was undeveloped. Though modern technology makes it possible, those with direct knowledge are the people engaged in practice throughout the world. They acquire knowledge through practice and transmit it through writing and technology to the scholar, who can then indirectly “know all that happens under heaven.”[^12]

This passage is a useful reminder to me.

Reading must of course continue when learning a language and studying a country. But if I remain entirely within books, papers and webpages, my understanding of local society will still be indirect. Language needs to be used in communication, evidence assessed through comparison, and conclusions from books tested against reality.

Perhaps one day I will have half a foot inside the field of China–Sri Lanka relations. Entering it will require more reading, as well as contact with real people, language and society.

Wherever my abilities eventually take me, I hope knowledge and practice can go together.
